\begin{abstract}
Intradialytic hypotension (IDH) is among the most frequent complications of haemodialysis. Clinical closed-loop systems that adjust the ultrafiltration rate (UFR) already exist, but they depend on optical relative-blood-volume sensors that a wearable or home device cannot easily carry. This project asks whether routinely recorded dialysis-machine data alone can support a predict-then-adjust control loop, and what that loop costs. Using HEMOBP (1{,}072 patients, 197{,}269 reconstructed sessions, 4.37M intra-session readings, CC~BY~4.0), we predict IDH in the remainder of a session from information available up to a decision point $T$, with patient-level group splits and AUPRC as the primary metric. Five models are compared (XGBoost, MLP, a 1D-CNN+MLP fusion, LSTM, and a from-scratch Transformer encoder). The best 60-minute model reaches AUPRC~0.2015 against a 0.0586 prevalence baseline (AUROC~0.774). A modality ablation shows that intra-session blood-pressure history is the only indispensable modality; removing it costs 44\% of AUPRC, while machine settings add almost no independent signal. Simulating three ultrafiltration policies in silico, a predictive policy lowers mean predicted IDH risk from 0.238 (constant UFR) to 0.192, but increases unremoved fluid from 699~mL to 957~mL and requires roughly 104 extra minutes. A perturbation analysis shows a monotone dose--response between UFR and risk, yet this gradient is equally consistent with confounding by indication, so all control gains must be read as upper bounds. We conclude that the software loop is buildable and evaluable offline, that sensor priority should go to continuous physiological monitoring, and that validating the control policy itself requires prospective data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Formulation}

Haemodialysis replaces kidney function intermittently. Between sessions, patients accumulate fluid; during a four-hour session a machine must remove that fluid at a rate the cardiovascular system can tolerate. When the ultrafiltration rate (UFR) exceeds the capillary refill rate, effective circulating volume falls and intradialytic hypotension (IDH) follows. In our cohort, 11.2\% of sessions reach a nadir systolic blood pressure (SBP) below 90~mmHg.

Three generations of countermeasures exist. First, reactive control: a proportional--integral--derivative (PID) controller, or nursing judgement, reacts only after blood pressure has already fallen. Second, blood-volume biofeedback: commercial systems such as Hemocontrol (Baxter) and the BVM/BTM-based feedback of Fresenius adjust UFR and dialysate conductivity in closed loop, guided by an optical sensor that measures relative blood volume \citep{santoro1998biofeedback,ronco2000biofeedback,dong2024closedloop}. Third, machine-learning prediction, which forecasts IDH from clinical variables but has rarely been evaluated as a component of a control loop \citep{bi2022lstm,idhmstf2025}.

The second generation works, but it pays for its sensor: optical relative-blood-volume hardware is bulky and expensive, and a wearable artificial kidney cannot easily host it. This motivates the question we study here: \emph{with routinely recorded machine data only --- pump settings, dialysate parameters, and the sparse blood-pressure readings nurses already take --- can a predict-then-adjust loop be built, and what does it cost?}

We decompose this into three testable sub-questions, each mapped to a claim from an accompanying engineering design project: (i) how early and how accurately can IDH be predicted (claim C1/C2); (ii) is multi-modal sensing necessary, and which modality matters (C3); (iii) how much IDH reduction does a predictive policy buy, and at what price in fluid removal and session time (C4/C5) --- with a fourth, honest question about whether observational data can support causal claims at all (C6).

The work is deliberately positioned against the course material: irregular EHR time series (Lecture~5, \textasciitilde 00:38), recurrent models (Lecture~6, \textasciitilde 02:14), the Transformer (Lecture~7, \textasciitilde 00:27 and \textasciitilde 27:16), multi-modal fusion (Lecture~8, \textasciitilde 00:11), the reinforcement-learning view of sequential decisions (Lecture~10, \textasciitilde 00:27), interpretability (Lecture~11, \textasciitilde 05:11) and privacy in federated deployments (Lecture~12, \textasciitilde 00:39) \citep{md6329lectures}. Software 3.0 (generative AI) was used early here, to survey the closed-loop ultrafiltration literature and to test whether the design project's "prediction beats PID" framing was defensible. It was not (Section~2), and correcting that framing changed the research question rather than its wording.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Closed-loop ultrafiltration.} Santoro et al. introduced a multivariable closed-loop controller that adjusts UFR and dialysate conductivity to follow a target blood-volume trajectory, cutting severe hypotensive episodes from 26 to 3 in a crossover study \citep{santoro1998biofeedback}. Ronco et al. found fewer hypotensive episodes and better equilibrated Kt/V under biofeedback \citep{ronco2000biofeedback}. A recent narrative review located 28 clinical studies, of which 23 reported effectiveness and none reported adverse effects, while concluding that evidence for clinical adoption still requires randomised trials \citep{dong2024closedloop}. Our work does not compete with these systems; it asks what can be done when their sensor is unavailable.

\paragraph{IDH definitions and epidemiology.} Prevalence depends heavily on definition \citep{kuipers2019prevalence}, and different definitions carry different mortality associations \citep{flythe2015idh}. Guidelines recommend keeping the ultrafiltration rate below roughly 13~mL/kg/h, alongside ultrafiltration profiling and cooler dialysate \citep{kdoqi2005cvd,china2022consensus}. We therefore report three definitions and justify the one we adopt.

\paragraph{Machine learning for IDH.} Tree ensembles and recurrent networks dominate the recent literature; a bidirectional LSTM reached 78.5\% accuracy and AUC 0.822 on 73{,}323 sessions \citep{bi2022lstm}, and a multi-scale temporal feature model reported 98.1\% AUC with XGBoost on a public cohort \citep{idhmstf2025}. A Temporal Fusion Transformer reached AUC 0.953 but at high compute cost. Two gaps motivate our design: prediction is rarely coupled to a control evaluation, and the causal status of the ultrafiltration signal is rarely examined. We address both, and we quantify the fluid-removal price of acting on a prediction. Our modelling choices also follow the course treatment of irregular clinical time series, recurrent gating, and attention over short sequences \citep{md6329lectures}.

\section{Data}

\paragraph{Cohort.} HEMOBP \citep{lin2019hemobp} contains dialysis-machine recordings linked to hospital demographic data, released under CC~BY~4.0: 1{,}072 patients, 4{,}366{,}298 intra-session readings. Sessions are not a calendar day --- the unit runs three shifts and the last crosses midnight --- so we reconstruct sessions as contiguous runs of readings separated by more than six hours, and assign each to a dialysis day beginning at 08:00. This yields 197{,}269 sessions (82.7\% of which match session-level weight records).

\paragraph{Descriptive statistics.} Mean session duration is 236 $\pm$ 24.5 minutes; mean UFR is 8.26 $\pm$ 3.38~mL/kg/h (median 7.8), i.e.\ mostly below the guideline threshold of 13; median interdialytic weight gain is 2.32~kg. Crucially, blood pressure is sparse: the median within-session interval between distinct readings is 50.7 minutes, and only 32\% of intervals are at or below 20 minutes (Figure~\ref{fig:eda}).

\paragraph{Sampling design.} For each session we form one sample per decision point $T \in \{30,60,90,120\}$ minutes. Inputs use only readings with time $\le T$; the label is the nadir SBP of readings after $T$. Sessions already below 90~mmHg before $T$ are excluded, since the prediction would be moot; sessions with fewer than three pre-$T$ readings are excluded. This yields 143{,}575 (T=30), 147{,}044 (T=60), 150{,}761 (T=90) and 151{,}182 (T=120) samples, with remaining-window IDH rates of 8.0\%, 7.7\%, 7.1\% and 6.5\% respectively. As a qualitative example, take one test session at $T$=60~min: 1.88~L of interdialytic gain, 0.46~L already removed, 1.42~L still to remove, 2.42~h remaining, and a clinician-chosen 0.48~L/h for the remainder. That plan removes 1.16~L and therefore leaves 266~mL unremoved, while SBP drifts from 148 to a nadir of 110~mmHg and no IDH event occurs. Sessions like this are where the label becomes noisy at the margin, and they are also where the fluid trade-off in E5 bites. Cleaning follows pre-registered bounds (duration 60--480~min, weight 20--200~kg, interdialytic gain $-2$ to 10~kg, UFR 0--20~mL/kg/h): 197{,}269 sessions reduce to 160{,}069 eligible.

\paragraph{Limitations of the data.} HEMOBP is \emph{in-centre} haemodialysis: four hours, supine, with sparse cuff measurements. It contains no photoplethysmography, electrocardiography, inertial or relative-blood-volume signals, and no symptom or nursing-intervention records. It therefore cannot validate a five-to-fifteen-minute warning window, and it cannot represent the ambulatory wearable scenario described in the accompanying design project. We return to this mismatch in the discussion.

\section{Approach}

\paragraph{Framing.} The primary task is classification: given the state at $T$, will this session reach a nadir SBP below 90~mmHg afterwards? Trajectory prediction appears only \emph{inside} the control simulation (Section~\ref{sec:e5}), where a dynamics model must answer ``what if the remaining ultrafiltration profile were different?'' Reporting classification as the main result keeps the headline numbers interpretable and avoids over-claiming.

\paragraph{Features and modalities.} Fifteen features fall into three groups, which we later ablate: (a) \emph{demographic/static} --- age, sex, diabetes, start weight, dry weight, interdialytic weight gain; (b) \emph{pre-$T$ physiology} --- first SBP, nadir so far, SBP slope, count of distinct readings; (c) \emph{machine settings} --- mean UFR, blood flow, dialysate conductivity and temperature up to $T$. Sequence models additionally consume up to 96 readings of seven channels (SBP, DBP, UFR, blood flow, conductivity, dialysate temperature, normalised elapsed time) with an explicit padding mask.

\paragraph{Models.} Five models are compared. (1) XGBoost \citep{chen2016xgboost} on tabular features is the clinical baseline. (2) A multilayer perceptron consumes static features only. (3) A 1D-CNN+MLP fusion mirrors the architecture of the accompanying design project: a convolutional branch over the reading sequence, an MLP branch over static features, concatenated before a shared head. (4) An LSTM \citep{hochreiter1997lstm} reads the masked sequence and takes the last valid hidden state. (5) A Transformer encoder built from scratch reuses the encoder written for the course assignment on clinical sequences \citep{md6329assignment}: sinusoidal positional encoding, multi-head scaled dot-product attention with key-padding masks, GELU feed-forward blocks and mean pooling over valid positions \citep{vaswani2017attention}.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} Splits are by patient (70/15/15) with a fixed seed, because a patient contributes dozens of sessions and row-wise splitting would leak. AUPRC is the primary metric given class imbalance; AUROC, Brier score and sensitivity at 80\% specificity are secondary. Because AUPRC is prevalence-sensitive, we report test prevalence alongside every value.

\paragraph{Training procedure.} All four neural models share one recipe, so that E1 isolates architecture rather than tuning. Inputs are standardised with training-split statistics only: each sequence channel is centred and scaled over valid (non-pad) training entries and re-zeroed at padded positions, and the fifteen static features are standardised with training-split mean and standard deviation. Training minimises binary cross-entropy with \texttt{pos\_weight} set to the training-split negative-to-positive ratio (17.1:1 at $T$=60); optimisation is Adam with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and batch size 256, for at most ten epochs, with early stopping of patience two epochs on validation AUPRC and restoration of the best-validation checkpoint before test evaluation. Dropout is 0.2 in the fusion and head layers and 0.1 inside the Transformer encoder, which uses $d$=64, four heads and two layers. Python, NumPy and PyTorch seeds are all fixed to 42. XGBoost uses the histogram tree method, 600 trees of depth 6, learning rate 0.05, 0.8 row and column subsampling, and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} equal to the same ratio. These defaults are revisited in E7.

\paragraph{Control policies (in silico).}\label{sec:e5}
We train a \emph{dynamics model} that adds two features to the tabular set: the mean UFR actually applied after $T$, and the remaining minutes. It is trained on clinicians' observed choices --- which is precisely why it is a simulator, not an estimator of causal effect. On the test set we then compare three policies at the decision point: \emph{constant} (keep the observed profile), \emph{reactive} (a rule-based proxy for biofeedback: cut UFR by 30\% if SBP has dropped $\ge$15~mmHg or nadir is below 100), and \emph{predictive} (choose from a candidate grid of $0.6$--$1.0\times$ the observed UFR, minimising predicted risk subject to a residual-fluid tolerance). For each policy we report mean predicted risk, mean unremoved fluid, and the extra minutes needed to still reach the prescribed fluid target.

\paragraph{Causal diagnosis.} Two probes test whether the ultrafiltration signal is causally usable. First, a perturbation sweep: we vary the planned remaining UFR multiplier from 0.3 to 1.3 and record mean predicted risk, plus the crude observed association between actual UFR and IDH. Second, an intervention-style ablation on the Transformer: zeroing the attention output or the feed-forward output of each encoder block (the intervention tooling follows \citealp{wu2024pyvene}) and measuring the change on the logit scale (not the probability scale, which saturates). Figure~\ref{fig:e6} shows the perturbation and association results.

\paragraph{Software 3.0.} Generative AI assistance was used throughout, under the course honour code, and is disclosed here. It was most valuable in three places: refactoring the session builder after pandas-groupby iteration failed on 160k sessions (a vectorised rewrite cut runtime from $>$20~min to 19~min for four horizons); diagnosing a misleading ablation result in an earlier assignment that turned out to be sigmoid saturation rather than a modelling failure; and localising a device-placement bug in a diagnostic model. It was least reliable on domain judgement --- choosing the IDH definition, deciding what the control comparison may legitimately claim, and recognising that the biofeedback literature makes ``predictive versus PID'' the wrong framing. All numerical results in this report were produced by scripts in the accompanying repository and can be regenerated.

\paragraph{E3: prediction horizon.} Table~\ref{tab:e3} shows a non-monotone pattern: quality peaks at $T$=90~min for the fusion model (AUPRC 0.2395, AUROC 0.807). \paragraph{E4: modality ablation.} Table~\ref{tab:e4} answers the sensor question. Pre-$T$ blood-pressure history alone nearly matches the full feature set (0.1592 versus 0.1675), and removing it costs 44\% of AUPRC --- the largest single loss. \paragraph{E6: is the ultrafiltration signal causally usable?} Figure~\ref{fig:e6} reports both probes. The perturbation sweep is monotone and in the expected direction: raising the planned remaining UFR multiplier from 0.3 to 1.3 raises mean predicted risk from 0.183 to 0.265 (gradient $+0.089$). The crude association agrees: binned by actual post-decision UFR, observed IDH rises from 3.1\% to 11.8\%. This is necessary for the control simulation to make sense --- but it is not sufficient to establish causality, because confounding by indication predicts the same pattern: patients who are more fluid-overloaded, and therefore more fragile, receive higher ultrafiltration and are also more likely to become hypotensive. The gradient's existence licenses the simulation; its magnitude cannot be decomposed into causal and confounded parts from this data.

A second probe is more surprising. Zeroing the attention output of each Transformer encoder block changes the logit by about $10^{-8}$ --- effectively nothing --- whereas zeroing the feed-forward output changes it by 0.042 and 0.067 at layers 0 and 1. The predictive signal therefore flows through the feed-forward path, not through attention, over a sequence of roughly 22 readings with sparse blood pressure. Two consequences follow: attention-heavy architectures are hard to justify here (reinforcing E1/E2), and attention maps must not be presented as clinical explanations --- the same observation-versus-intervention lesson from our earlier coursework.

\paragraph{E7: hyperparameter selection.} The brief asks for training and hyperparameter selection, so we swept the two choices with the largest effect. For XGBoost, tree depth dominates: AUPRC peaks at depth 3 (0.1904 with 600 trees) and falls monotonically to 0.1675 at depth 6 and 0.1520 at depth 9, while adding trees beyond 600 does not help (Figure~\ref{fig:e7}). With roughly 100k training rows and a signal concentrated in a few physiological features, deeper trees overfit. For the 1D-CNN+MLP, the learning rate matters more than the class-weight correction: AUPRC rises from 0.1817 at $10^{-3}$ to 0.1926 at $3\times10^{-4}$ and falls to 0.1696 at $3\times10^{-3}$, while disabling the positive-class weight costs about five relative percent. Retuning therefore changes the E1 ordering at the margin: XGBoost moves from 0.1675 to 0.1904 and the fusion model from 0.1837 to 0.1926, leaving the from-scratch Transformer (0.2015) ahead by a much smaller margin than E1 suggests. We keep E1 on identical default settings because that comparison holds the pipeline constant, and report the tuned numbers as evidence that architecture ranking at this signal strength is sensitive to tuning --- a caution for anyone deploying these models.

\paragraph{Software 3.0 in the experimental workflow.} Two of the seven experiments exist because of an AI-assisted check rather than a planned one: the probability-versus-logit diagnosis that produced the whole-test-set sanity check, and the suggestion to test attention against feed-forward ablation once the attention heatmaps looked uniform. \section{Discussion and Conclusion}

\paragraph{What we can now say.} Three questions were asked at the outset. First, how early and how accurately: with routine machine data and sparse cuff pressures, IDH is predictable at a 60-minute horizon with AUPRC 0.2015 against 0.0586 prevalence (AUROC 0.774), and a lightweight fused architecture captures 91\% of that at a quarter of the parameters and 44$\times$ less batch latency. Second, whether multi-modal sensing is necessary: only intra-session blood pressure is indispensable; machine settings contribute almost no independent signal at $T$=60. Third, what prediction buys and costs: 19.5\% relative risk reduction in silico, paid for with 258~mL more unremoved fluid and roughly 104 extra minutes.

\paragraph{Boundaries we will not overstep.} Every control number here is in silico under a model trained on clinicians' own decisions; it is not clinical validation. The dose--response gradient is equally compatible with confounding by indication, so the E5 gains are upper bounds. The attention-ablation result means explanation should not be read off attention weights. And the cohort is in-centre haemodialysis with a cuff measurement every 50.7 minutes: it cannot speak to the ambulatory wearable scenario, which lacks exactly the high-frequency modalities (photoplethysmography, inertial, pressure waveforms) whose value our ablation implies. Scaling from 4-hour supine sessions to 6--8-hour ambulatory therapy, and from sparse to continuous sensing, remains an extrapolation that this dataset cannot adjudicate.

\paragraph{Implications for the design project.} The results strengthen three claims and weaken one. Strengthened: continuous physiological sensing deserves the sensor budget (E4); a compact convolutional-plus-MLP model is the right deployment shape (E1/E2); and a clinician-set safety envelope with a hard physical override is not bureaucratic decoration but a necessary condition, precisely because the causal strength of ultrafiltration cannot be established retrospectively (E6). Weakened: the design project's framing of its contribution as ``prediction beats PID'' is historically inaccurate --- 28 clinical studies of closed-loop ultrafiltration biofeedback predate it \citep{dong2024closedloop}. The defensible framing is narrower and stronger: \emph{replacing the optical relative-blood-volume sensor with routine machine data and inference}. The five-to-fifteen-minute warning specification also needs restating, since it is not verifiable on routine data and depends on the continuous sensing the device is meant to provide.

\paragraph{What the project taught me.} The value of a healthcare-AI contribution sits less in the highest AUPRC than in knowing which claim the data can carry. The most useful result here is a negative one --- that the control policy cannot be validated retrospectively --- and it took a controlled model comparison, a modality ablation and an intervention probe to establish it rather than assert it.

\paragraph{Future work.} Four directions follow directly. External validation on a second centre, since all results here come from one cohort. A calibrated deployment of the fusion model, as XGBoost remains better calibrated and clinical thresholds need probabilities, not ranks. A targeted-causal analysis --- within-patient comparisons or instrumental variables --- to bound the causal share of the ultrafiltration gradient. And a high-frequency simulation arm, calibrated to the distributions reported here, to test whether the ambulatory scenario preserves or reverses the conclusions.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} Sensor-light closed-loop ultrafiltration control is buildable as software and evaluable offline, and its central trade-off --- hypotension risk against fluid removal and session time --- can be quantified. What it cannot be, on retrospective routine data, is causally validated. Naming that boundary is not a weakness of the study; it is the finding that should shape the next experiment.
